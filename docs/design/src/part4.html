15

Art

Clean anime. Heroes and creatures are cel-shaded sprites, painted once per view by an image model and set in motion by a video model, so they move like drawings and stay the same character from every direction. Everything they touch, throw, orbit, pick up or stand on is 3D. Icons are one bold object each with a black outline, made to read at 72 px on a phone. All of it is generated on the local art server from prompts that live in the content files.

What is 3D and what is a sprite

ThingMade asWhy
Heroes, creatures, bosses2.5D sprite camera-facing billboard pivoting on the feet, five painted views, mirrored to eight directionsPainted faces read better than low-poly ones, and a view per direction keeps the character honest from the high camera.
Weapons, familiars and their effects3D mesh + particlesSaw blades on a tilted orbit, a grenade on a real arc, a pea pod with flapping leaves. Sprites of these would look flat and wrong from above.
Pickups: gems, coins, chests, power-ups3D mesh spinning; power-ups a billboard icon on a plinthCheap, readable from any angle, hundreds on screen through a MultiMesh.
Terrain, landmarks, structures, skybox3D low-poly, paintedThe ground is 3D by definition; the big landmarks and the structures come from Blender.
Scenery: cacti, rocks, bones, dead trees, signs2.5D sprite one painting each, base-pivot billboards in a MultiMeshThey match the cast's line, cost one draw call per kind, and hundreds of them scatter for nothing. Muted so they read as background.
Telegraphs, progress wedges, poison clouds, spit trailsdraped meshes on the groundThey must hug dunes and mesa edges; a flat disc cuts through a slope.
Icons, cards, HUD, menus2DScreen-space UI.
Shadows under characters3D blobGrounds a flat sprite in a 3D world. The single most important trick for 2.5D.

The hero's sprite never holds a weapon. Weapons float at marker points around the character: a hip point for the shotgun, a ring for the saws, an orbit for the pea pod and the dart. That keeps the sprite one image per pose and lets any weapon pair with any hero.

Sprite animation

The skeletons were pose-driven too (v0.23.4, shelved with them in v0.23.5): a creature JSON with "pose" names a driver folder and gen_art.py paints its front and back walks on it instead of the free clip; the skeletons' driver is the Mixamo walk with both arms pinned forward by damped-track constraints (tools/pose_driver.py ... arms=zombie), so they come with their arms out like the dead.

Icons to choose from (v0.23.4). The launcher icon is the Drifter's hat lying on the sand (variant A, the plainest: the hat big on soft dunes, picked in v0.23.5 over the hat against a low sun), and his character icon a serious portrait (variant B); both came as four variants each (tools/gen_icon_variants.py, Flux.2 dev with the style anchor and his front painting as references, laid out on two lettered sheets for picking on a phone) and --use hat=X drifter=Y installs a pick. The lessons of the earlier icons are in its prompts: the subject inside the middle two thirds for the launcher's mask, no text or frame (the painted rounded frame the model adds is cropped off), the portrait edited from the hero's own painting so the face holds, and nothing named that the model would draw literally.

Heroes are pose-driven. A Mixamo motion (a run, a pain gesture) is rendered as an OpenPose skeleton with hands from each of the five game views in Blender (tools/pose_driver.py), and Wan 2.2 Animate moves the painted still along it. Every driver frame is a sprite frame: 24 frames per stride at 36 fps, frame rates carried in the hero's JSON. Animate paints the body on the driver's nose-to-ankle skeleton, which came out about a head taller than the painted still, so every animated frame is measured and fitted to the standing figure's height before it is packed; idle, run and hit now share one head height. The method, with before-and-after clips of the Drifter, has its own page.

The hit reaction is painted, not driven. The Mixamo pain gesture never looked right on any hero, so the hurt pose is a still per view: the image model edits the view's own painting into a flinch (leaning back, shoulders hunched, a hand to the chest, and for Astro the visor kept opaque), it is fitted to the standing height, and four frames at 12 fps wince around it (the flinch, a deeper lean, the flinch, half a step back to standing). It reads like the rest of the sheet because it is made the same way.

Everyone dies on screen. Every creature, the boss, the Elite and every hero have a painted "dead" still (the image model lays the front painting flat on its back, eyes shut, limbs sprawled) and three frames of the standing painting tipping over into it, at 8 fps; a body holds for a moment and sinks into the sand, 1.4 seconds from the killing blow to gone. Bodies stop blocking shots and steering the instant they fall. The goblin and the runt really fall (v0.23.9, "death": "clip" in their JSON): Wan 2.2 first-last-frame animates the fall from the standing painting to the dead one, and eight frames of that clip play at 12 fps.

Creatures use free clips. Ants, cacti, beetles, mummies and the tumbleweed have no human skeleton, so their front and back stills are asked to walk in place by Wan 2.2 image-to-video, the loop is found by comparing silhouettes and twelve frames are cut at 15 fps. Chargers get three more stills (side, both diagonals, edited from the front painting) and a clip each. The goblin and the runt moved to humanoid drivers in v0.23.9: Mixamo's orc walk for the goblin and its plain walk for the quicker runt, each refitted to a big head and short legs (pose_driver.py body=..., below); the goblin specials and the mummy could follow.

Procedural layer on top, no frames needed: bob while moving, lean into turns, scale-pop on spawn, squash on hit with a white flash (green-white when the hit is poison), a green tint while poisoned, wobble when tossed, shrink on death, and a shadow that shrinks with height. These come from the shader and a few lines of code. Strips are grids (strip_cols in the JSON, read by both billboard shaders): a hero strip is 153 cells of 256 px, a charger's 83 cells of 192.

Painted scenery, and reading through it

How they sit in the world. Each prop is an instance in one MultiMesh per sprite (core/systems/PropField.gd), a quad pivoting on its base that faces the camera exactly like the creatures do (prop_billboard.gdshader). Props and characters are both drawn opaque with an alpha cut rather than blended, so the depth buffer sorts them per pixel: a goblin standing in front of a cactus covers it, one behind it is covered, and because every quad is tilted the same way towards the lens the planes are parallel and never cut through each other, so there is no z-fighting to manage. Greenery sways a few degrees at the top in the vertex shader; rocks and bones do not.

Pipeline

  1. Prompt. Every content JSON carries an art_prompt (and heroes a portrait_prompt, anim_hint and anim_negative). The script wraps it in the house style.
  2. Stills. ComfyUI on the R9700 server runs Flux.2 Klein 9B (4 steps, about 20 s a render): a front view for everything, then a back view (and for heroes and chargers a side and two diagonals) edited from the front so the character stays the same.
  3. Motion. Heroes: pose drivers into Wan 2.2 Animate (about a minute a clip). Creatures: Wan 2.2 image-to-video, the loop period found from silhouettes (about two minutes a clip).
  4. Check. LM Studio's vision model grades every render (one subject, not cropped, no text, flat background, right view) and failures re-roll with a new seed up to three times; the grade is kept in <id>_ai.json.
  5. Cut. Key the green screen, drop specks and ground shadows, lift contrast, add the outline (heavier on creatures than on heroes), fit the cell, pack the grid strip, write the frame counts and rates back into the JSON. Hero portraits are cropped from an edit of the front still and set in a round badge.
  6. Review. tools/dl/review/ gets an icon sheet, a strip sheet and every sprite at its on-screen size on the sand texture.
  7. The creature table. Every enemy JSON carries a doc line; the bible's creature table is generated from the content folders and the map's roster at build time, so a creature cannot be added without appearing here.
  8. Pose hints. A hero's stills decide his cycles: the pose-driven run keeps whatever the still's arms were doing (Buck's fists stayed at his chest, Dad's back view showed his face). A character's JSON may now carry pose_hint (every standing view), back_hint and side_hint, appended to the view prompts; the rule is arms hanging, nothing raised, no face from behind, and the same build in every view. The vision QA does not catch a face-forward back view, so every re-rendered still is looked at before the sheet packs.
  9. The launcher icon. Until v0.13 it was Astro's portrait badge on a gradient, and at launcher size her mirrored visor read as a black disc: no face, no game. tools/gen_app_icon.py now paints it as one illustration on Flux.2 dev from her front still and the style anchor: chest up, the thumbs-up glove as a second read, the visor reflecting a sun and a cactus so it is bright instead of black, a sun halo behind the helmet so the white suit pops off the warm sky, saguaro silhouettes at the corners, the subject inside the middle of the frame for the adaptive safe zone. Candidates are rendered per seed and one is picked by eye; the model's own painted frame is cropped away.
  10. Scenery. tools/gen_props.py paints the scenery, the trees and bushes and the seven structures (each with a spent version, an edit of the lit painting with the life prompted out of it) from the anchor, keys, outlines thinly, mutes and writes art/sprites/props/props.json with each prop's height and collider radius (the radius is 62% of the painted foot since v0.13, so the hero brushes past a cactus instead of bouncing off its air, and creatures steer around the foot and not a halo of it); the map reads that manifest. Landmarks are modelled in Blender by script and exported as GLB; textures for sand, cracked earth, sandstone and the sky panorama are generated by tools/gen_textures.py.

One creature costs about six minutes of server time; a charger about fifteen; a hero about twenty. python tools/gen_art.py --only coyote does the whole thing and imports the result into Godot. Section 15b walks through it step by step, with the pictures each step made.

15b

How the art is made

Nobody draws the cast by hand. Every hero, creature and icon starts as one sentence in its data file. AI models on our own art server paint it, a second AI that can see checks each picture, a Python script cuts it out and outlines it, and it lands in Godot as a sheet of frames. Below is the kit, then four worked examples from the game (a Goblin, the Drifter, the Big Mouth and the Tommy Gun) and five shorter ones. Section 15 has the rules the art follows; this is the kitchen.

Why green and blue backgrounds

Every still is painted on a flat, bright screen colour that the scissors can take away cleanly, like a film's green screen. Green things (goblins, cacti) go on blue, blue things on green, and heroes always on blue, because olive, khaki and brown clothes sit too close to green. For the video clips the script picks whichever screen is furthest from the figure's own colours: a hero in blue jeans came back from the video model with no legs on blue and green legs on green, so he goes on magenta.

One sentence becomes a prompt

The data file only says what the thing is. The script wraps that sentence in everything else the painter needs to hear, the same way for every item, so changing the look of the whole cast means changing one paragraph in gen_art.py, not forty prompts. This is the Goblin's front view, exactly as it was sent:

Using exactly the same art style, rendering, shading, palette and outline treatment as the reference image, draw a new subject: a small green goblin with big pointed ears, a hooked nose, sharp little teeth in a wicked grin, wearing a ragged brown loincloth and a leather belt, holding a crude wooden club, standing and facing the viewer. Full body, front view, standing and facing the viewer. In a clean modern anime art style: crisp confident line art, cel shading with two-tone shadows and sharp highlights, flat saturated colors, appealing stylized proportions, simple readable shapes with no texture noise, like character art from a Japanese action RPG mobile game. Game asset. Isolated on a flat solid bright blue (#0000FF) background that fills the entire frame, floating in empty space with no ground plane, no cast shadow, no grass, no text, no watermark. Do not copy any subject from the reference.
the style anchor goes along as a reference picturethe item's own sentence (art_prompt)the viewthe house stylethe screen

The style anchor (section 15) is the one picture that goes with every hero and creature render, and it is why a goblin, a lizard prophet and a man in a duster look like one game. Icons have a house style of their own instead ("a mobile game item icon in the style of Shop Titans: one single bold object, thick clean black outline...") and take no anchor.

How a Goblin is made

The Goblin is the crowd: 1.2 m tall, drawn in a 128-pixel square, and everything else is measured against it. A creature gets two painted views, a walk for each and a death. Since v0.23.9 the goblin is made the way the heroes are: it walks on a borrowed Mixamo stride, fitted to its own body, and its death is a real fall. About ten minutes of server time, plus a person's eye on every picture.

The Vulture Raider (the Rocketeer's new look) is the other recipe of the pass: a creature copied from a picture. v0.35 (pass 49, Garry: "a lot of the feet stay still while he is supposed to be moving"): BB-B and BB-C walked again on the same paintings. Two things had kept the legs still. Blender's default view transform (AgX) squeezed every depth video into one narrow band of grey, so Wan was walking a flat silhouette: the far legs, painted below the body, had nothing to follow, and from the front a leg swinging towards the camera changed nothing at all. The depth is now written Raw over each view's own range (blender_beetle.py depth=tight), near legs bright and far legs dark. And a real beetle's step is small: at 2 m long seen from 22 m it reads as a glide, so every leg bone now swings 1.7 times as far round its own average pose (amp=1.7), the body and the horns as the clip has them. The walks were measured as well as looked at: the area under the shell changes from frame to frame in every view.

v0.36 (pass 50, Garry kept BB-C): code animation over the painted frames. A charge is too short and too fast to paint, so the beetle's charge is drawn by the game on top of its walk: the frames run faster, the billboard squashes, stretches, shakes, bounces and rolls about its feet (the creature shaders read a roll off the instance's basis), and the dust is a new soft puff in the effects (a camera-facing disc frayed by the flames' noise, paler on top), where the older sand puffs are see-through spheres. Two more checks came with them. A colour that creeps into a clip and stays for a few frames (a club turning magenta, a fall smeared into a rainbow) is measured against the painting itself: the share of the figure in hues the painting does not have (gen_art.hue_foreign). Bad clips scored over 0.05 and every good one in the game stays under 0.016, so a clip over 0.025 is painted again and a fall frame over it gives way to its neighbour. And a walk keeps frames 2 to 13 of its clip instead of 1 to 12 (the last repeats the first pose, so it is the same loop): nearly every clip rolled again for a one-frame flash had it on frame 1. How Solenne was made (v0.40)

Garry handed over a turnaround of the sun-headed woman, front and back on transparency, and a Mixamo walk he picked for her (Walking_sun.fbx, one stride with a sway and both arms swinging wide). The front half of the picture is her title painting and her badge as it is (tools/make_solenne_static.py: Garry liked the art, so nothing was painted over it), and the two halves laid on the blue screen are her front and back stills; Klein painted the side and the three-quarter views as edits of the front, the hurt stills, the cheer and the fall. The walk became a pose driver like the heroes' runs (pose_driver.py, 24 frames, with the back views' wrists pushed clear of the body by pose_spread.py), and Wan Animate walked every view on it.

Three things went wrong and were fixed in the tools. Her arms swing so wide that the back and three-quarter-back figures touched the clip's edges: six tries failed and one kept a boot the keyer cut off, so those two views' drivers were shrunk to 85 % about the figure's middle (tools/pose_scale.py; the frames are scaled back to her height when packed). Her glossy black latex reflects the screen, and the keyer cut those reflections out as screen, leaving hollow boots and floating heels; three keyer settings in her JSON fix it at the source: a screen-coloured pixel darker than 0.8 is figure (key_min_value, with key_sat 0.85 keeping the paler highlights), there is no cast-shadow cut (key_cut_shadow false; a clip has no shadow), and the screen's colour is held down in every figure pixel instead of unmixed (key_clamp_screen), so a reflection turns into grey shine. And the back-view hurt still turned her to face the camera, as Ironwood's had: it was painted again as an edit of her back still, flinching from behind, four seeds, one picked.

Her walks, again (v0.41)

Garry found the first walks wrong: walking down, her hands were too large and her sun head grew and looked aside; walking up it shrank; walking left or right she seemed to walk up the screen with her rays split in two. Two causes. The stick figure was a man's from the clip, a third taller in the body than her painted body (her head and its rays take that height), so the video model painted her on his proportions and settled the head's size differently in each view; and the clip's forward was read off its feet, which a walk with the toes turned out puts 20 degrees wrong, so every view was turned and the side view showed her a quarter turned away.

The fix is in the tools. tools/pose_driver.py ... fwd=body squares the views to her hips and shoulders. tools/pose_fit.py then scales the stick figure to her painted body as the clip sees it (the neck point to the ankles, from tools/pose_templates/solenne.json), pins the face on her painted sun disc so it faces the viewer in every view and never tilts or turns, and makes the hands smaller; the back views still go through pose_spread, so no hand is painted on her back. Her side still is composed, the side painting's body under the three-quarter-front painting's head, because the image model drew the disc edge-on six times out of six. On those drivers she walks three ways, as art variants Garry picks from in the arena: B on his walk, C on Mixamo's catwalk, D on the heroes' run; B plays until he chooses.

Checking, and painting again

Everything is kept: each raw picture in tools/dl/ai_raw_anime/ next to its grade, and each clip in tools/dl/ai_anim/ with a contact sheet of its loop. Painting one thing again is one line:

  • python tools/gen_art.py --only goblin makes or finishes one item and imports it into Godot.
  • --force --seed=2 paints everything for it again with new seeds; --keys=goblin_back paints just one picture again.
  • --dev paints the stills on Flux.2 dev instead of Klein.
  • python tools/animate.py still.png "running in place" 1 tries a clip on its own; delete its folder in tools/dl/ai_anim/ to make one again.
  • "$BLENDER" -b --python tools/pose_driver.py -- fbx=... out=... frames=24, then python tools/pose_driver.py draw <out>, makes new motion from any Mixamo clip.
  • python tools/comfy.py --export writes the workflows so they can be opened and played with in ComfyUI's own browser UI.

These pictures are cut from the pipeline's own files by tools/make_pipeline_figs.py.

Making art yourself: a step-by-step guide

The same guide as ART_ASSET_PIPELINE.md in the repo. Everything runs on the PC from a terminal in the repo folder; the game never reads a prompt, the prompts are what these scripts paint from.

you need
The ComfyUI art server (192.168.137.54:8188) switched on, Python, and Blender 5.2 for anything built from a 3D model. After new art, Godot re-imports it (open the editor, or --import then import_json.gd); bash tools/ship.sh does both before it builds.
the one rule
Look at it yourself. Every script paints several candidates and writes a review sheet (review_*.png): pick by eye, then install the pick. The vision check misses extra ears, arms, tails and missing legs.
the words
In the item's JSON: art_prompt (what is painted), back_hint, view_hints, dead_hint, pose_hint (the other views), motion, anim_negative, anim_seed (the walk clip), variants.<CODE>.recipe (the same, for one art variant), outline_frac, outline_color, remap_hues (clean-up). Edit the words, run the script again, pick again.
enemies
python tools/gen_art.py --only skeleton --force repaints one from its JSON (front, back, dead, walk, fall, strip and icon); --variant=SK-B paints one variant from its recipe, which you then compare in Debug · Arena → ENEMY → Art variants and switch to with "variant" in the JSON. Creatures with a 3D model (the scorpion, the fire ant) walk the model in Blender first and paint along its depth video: tools/blender_scorpion.py, then tools/gen_ctl.py --id=dune_scorpion --variant=SC-A (side candidates, then --use-side=k1); the ant has tools/gen_ant.py. Hopping creatures ("walk": "hop") need no video. A floating shadow is the JSON's shadow block, not a repaint. About 20 minutes a creature on Klein, up to three hours for a 3D-driven one on Flux dev.
heroes
python tools/gen_art.py --only drifter: five views, a run in each driven by a Mixamo stride (pose_drivers in the hero JSON picks one per view), hit and dead paintings, the portrait icon. Paint the front as 4-6 candidates first: every other view is an edit of it. The title painting: tools/gen_title_heroes.py --only drifter --seeds 4, then --pick drifter=2.
icons
python tools/gen_icon_cands.py --only beer --seeds 4, look at tools/dl/icons47/review_beer.png, then --pick beer=2 installs it into the item's folder. A new item is one entry in JOBS in that script. Anything green in the subject goes on the blue screen ("screen": "blue"), or the keyer eats it. Check each icon on its plate colour first. Weapons with a 3D model are painted from a render of it ("ref"). The same script paints badges, UI art (the coin) and the Results screen's source icons; the app icon has tools/gen_app_icon.py.
props and structures
python tools/gen_props.py --only st_teleport --force (Flux dev by default, --klein for the fast model). The list and sizes are PROPS in that script; a used structure has a second painting st_<id>_off.png. Structures stay paintings, never 3D.
UI art
python tools/gen_ui_art.py --only mirror --seeds=4 then --pick (the Stranger, the Strongbox, the portal, the Mirror); the chest frames are a closed painting and two edits of it (cracked, open); badges tools/gen_badges.py.
3D models
"$BLENDER" -b --python tools/blender_turret.py and the other blender_*.py scripts build the GLBs in game/art/props/, several from Garry's references in _REFERENCES/, each with a render the icon is painted from.
where it lands
Strips and icons in the content folder; raw paintings cached in tools/dl/ai_raw_anime/ (delete one to repaint it); clips in tools/dl/ai_anim/; review sheets in tools/dl/review/, tools/dl/icons47/, tools/dl/title_heroes/; replaced art in tools/dl/backup/.

Sound

Cartoon western: boings, splats and twangs, with a galloping soundtrack that adds a layer per stage. Every sound has an id that content files reference, a trigger, and a description written as a prompt for a sound AI. Placeholders are synthesised by script so the game is never silent while we wait for the real ones.

direction
Nothing realistic. Hits are "bonk" and "splat", deaths are comedic, UI is wooden and warm. Saturday-morning cartoon foley over a spaghetti-western guitar.
mixing rules
Voice limits per id, so forty fire ants dying is one sound with a pitch spread. Player and UI always audible. Creature sounds attenuate with distance from the camera. Everything ducks under the level-up sting.
placeholders
tools/gen_sfx.py writes a short WAV for every id: blips for UI, filtered noise bursts for hits, pitch sweeps for jumps and pickups, a looped chord for music. Ugly, but correct in timing and volume, and the sound map already points at the right ids. Voice barks come from Kokoro TTS.
music
Three run layers that come in per stage, a boss track, a title track, results; generated by tools/gen_music.py.
{{SOUND_ROWS}}
IdTriggerPrompt for a sound AI

The sound map that binds these ids to files is itself a content resource (content/sounds/soundmap.json), so swapping a placeholder for a real file is editing one row.

Tech & performance budgets

engine
Godot 4.7.2, GDScript. Compatibility renderer for the widest Android reach.
targets
Android first, on Garry's phone through a signed release APK dropped in Google Drive. PC for testing with WASD. 60 fps on a mid-range phone.
creatures
Budget of 60 alive by the end of a run (25 at the start), hard cap 80 during swarm rings; the arrays and MultiMeshes are sized for 320, so the cap is a design number, not an engine one. Logic is data-oriented: positions, velocities, HP, poison sources and frame index live in packed arrays, not one node per creature. Rendering is one MultiMeshInstance3D per creature type, billboarded in a shared shader, with the animation frame, flip, flash and squash as per-instance custom data.
movement
Creatures are not physics bodies. Separation steering on a spatial hash grid; each creature steers every other physics tick. The hero is one CharacterBody3D against terrain and static prop colliders. v0.23.8 (the late game on a phone): the grid is two flat arrays (each cell's first creature, each creature's next) rebuilt every tick instead of a dictionary of arrays, on the same cells and in the same order, so every query answers as before; half the crowd steers on each tick instead of all of it on every other one (the steering tick cost three times the one between, a judder on a phone); each creature checks only the obstacles whose circle reaches its cell instead of all hundred on the map; the camera's forward and each type's frame rates are read once a tick; and the draw picks each creature's strip from a table built once per type instead of building and looking up strip names for every creature every tick.
effects
Every burst, puff, flame, spark and flash is an instance in one of three pooled MultiMeshes (400 particles, three draw calls, no nodes). Lightning bolts are ribbons in two shared meshes, a coloured sheath and a white core (v0.23.8: two nodes, two materials and two draw calls per strike before). Point lights on chests, structures and pickups are skipped on phones, where every lit pixel pays for each of them; the campfire and the portal keep theirs. Phones render the 3D view at 80% and the UI at full size.
projectiles
Object pooled 3D meshes, 400 of them. Straight shots hold their muzzle height above the ground wherever they go; arcs ignore contact and explode where they land. Hit checks are sphere tests against the same grid, not physics queries. Hostile shots drop a fading trail dot every 55 ms into a second pool.
warm-up
The Compatibility renderer compiles a shader the first time something is drawn with it, which on a phone showed as a hitch whenever a new creature, weapon or effect first appeared. Under the spawn beam, everything the run can draw is drawn once in a pit eight metres under the hero for a third of a second (core/systems/Warmup.gd): every creature type and projectile kind through their MultiMeshes, every weapon visual, the effect pools, the ground-mark and cloud materials, chests, mines, power-ups, the portal, and the fonts at every size the overlays use.
scenery
Painted props are MultiMesh billboards (one per sprite kind, up to 200 instances each), opaque with an alpha cut so they depth-sort against creatures per pixel; the occluded-outline pass on creatures and the hero is a second material with an inverted depth test.
sprites
Every sprite faces the camera fully (a quad pivoting on its feet, tilted towards the lens), so nothing is seen edge-on from the high camera. The hero is one quad with the sprite_billboard shader; creatures use the MultiMesh twin with the same maths. Both read grid strips.
ground marks
Telegraph rings, lines, cones, blast rings, oil spills, poison puddles and their billows, zones and scorch marks drape over the dunes instead of cutting them. v0.23.8: each is one instance in a MultiMesh of a ready-made shape (a ring band, a disc, a strip, a lobed spill), laid over the terrain vertex by vertex by core/shaders/ground_mark.gdshader from the map's height grid (the same bilinear step as height_at), one draw call per kind of mark. Before, every mark was a node with its own material and a mesh built on the spot, and a blast ring was built again twenty times a second while it grew: an explosive build spent close to a millisecond a frame on it on a desktop and put 60 draw calls on the ground. The look is the same to the pixel (the old meshes carried every triangle twice, so the shader blends a ring, a disc, a cone or a line as twice-drawn); blast rings now grow smoothly every frame instead of in twenty steps a second. The structures' progress wedge is still built once.
draw calls
v0.23.8: creatures, shots and gems are drawn once per shown frame, after a physics tick has moved them (the draw used to run inside the tick, so a phone below 60 fps drew the crowd twice for every frame it showed). The orbiting saws are one MultiMesh (twenty nodes for an evolved Saw Halo); the wall's 36 posts are one MultiMesh; a weapon model's plain parts that share a material are merged into one mesh as it loads (core/systems/MeshMerge.gd: 142 draw calls down to 104 across the 40 weapon models, pixel for pixel the same; the parts the code moves by name are left alone) (the path emblems that hung over the weapons until v0.45 are gone). On a desktop, the benchmark's late fight (six evolved weapons, level 45, 150 creatures) went from about 7.8 ms a frame to 5.4 at 60 fps, and from 12.7 to 9 at 30 fps; draw calls from about 310 to 210.
terrain
One heightmap mesh, 140 m, with a height-blended two-texture shader. Collision from the heightmap. Mesas and ramps are separate modular meshes and report their own height.
save
One JSON save file: gold, shop tiers, unlocks, quest progress, settings, best runs, hint flags, one-time migrations.
audio
A sound map resource binds every id from section 16 to a file. Voice-limited pools per id.
testing
A parse check of every script and scene, 33 gdUnit4 tests, a perf mode of the smoke run (perf=1) that prints each system's milliseconds per frame and every frame over 33 ms with what it was doing, a late-game benchmark (tools/perf_late.gd, v0.23.8: a maxed build in the stage-3 crowd, each system's milliseconds and the draw calls every ten seconds, at 60 or 30 frames a second of game time), a headless smoke run that plays through two bosses, every structure, the reel, the Elite and every hub screen, and a silent screenshot pass that captures every screen for this document. bash tools/ship.sh runs the checks, builds the signed APK and drops it in Drive.

19

Open questions

Decisions taken so the build could exist. Each is a one-line change if it turns out wrong.

  • Death ends the run. One life, a revive as a meta unlock.
  • The hero lands with his own weapon; there is no starter pick (pass 45; before it, the pick happened every run).
  • Weapons are 3D, characters are sprites. The hero never visibly holds a weapon in the sprite.
  • Creature cap 25 to 60. The engine keeps its 320 budget so the cap can go back up in data.
  • Chests pause the game. Four seconds of reel per chest is the price of making a chest an event; the regular-creature odds are set so a run sees a handful, not a dozen.
  • Selling a chest pays a fifth of the shop price. Deliberately small; if gold ever matters more inside a run, this is the first number to raise.
  • Fire spreads at a flat 40% of the weapon's chance for every generation after the first. A decaying chain (20%, 8%, 3%) was the alternative; the flat one keeps big packs exciting and is one constant to change.
  • The Elite is one creature per map. A second mini-boss with a different brain (a spitter, say) would be a folder and one more event line.
  • Game name. "88fits" is the folder name and the working title.

Open questions that only more playing can answer: whether stage 2 is now a wall or a cliff, whether the coyote pack needs a sound of its own to be read before it arrives, whether the reel should ever offer a new weapon instead of a level, and how long endless stays interesting past minute twenty.
